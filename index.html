Research

Immigration, Internal Migration, and the Rental Market

Job Market Paper · Draft coming soon

Abstract

This paper investigates the effect of immigration on the housing market and how internal migration can amplify or offset these effects. I extend existing decompositions of the total effect of immigration by allowing natives' location choices to depend on immigration shocks in both their current locations and their prospective destinations. Even in the absence of a native response to immigration in other locations, the model shows that regions will still be affected by other regions' immigration shocks. Importantly, the magnitude of the response depends on the regional population size relative to the national population. I study this question empirically in the context of the Canadian rental market during a period of high immigration from 2015 to 2024. Using linked Canadian administrative data, I construct an instrument exploiting the rapid increase in international student arrivals in Canada following national-level policy changes. This work highlights the difficulty of designing place-based immigration policies in the presence of free mobility.

Faculty Unionization and University Funding

Working paper

Abstract

This paper studies the impact of faculty unionization in Canada on the university's funding. Using an event-study framework with staggered treatment timing, I consider the effects of unionization at 21 schools between 1981 and 2017 on sponsored research funding, contributions to the endowment, and revenue collected from fees. I find no effect following faculty unionization for primarily undergraduate universities (typically smaller schools with lower levels of research activity and few graduate programs). For comprehensive universities (which offer graduate programs and have some level of research activity), I find no effect on sponsored research funding, an increase in contributions to the endowment by 2% per year after ten years, and some evidence that revenue collected from fees decreases, although pre-trends prevent the proper identification of this effect. Finally, for medical doctoral universities (the largest, most research-intensive schools) I see no effect in their sponsored research funding, a decrease in contributions to their endowment, and an increase in the revenue they collect from fees. These results highlight the importance of allowing for heterogeneity by both institution type and funding source.

ECO220: Introduction to Data Analysis and Applied Econometrics

ECO220 is a second-year undergraduate course for economics and management students. It provides a comprehensive introduction to econometric methodology and data analysis, spanning four major sections: understanding data, probability, statistical inference, and regression. I taught the first term of the course, and coordinated assessments and teaching assistant duties, for each of the summers.
